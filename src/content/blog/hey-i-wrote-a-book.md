*The Lonely Ponderosa* was originally written as a screenplay ten or so years ago by me and my brother. We wrote it quickly and efficiently and it was my first and his I-don’t-know-how-many-eth.

I hope you enjoy reading the book. Below is its journey.

![Illustration for Hey I Wrote a Book](/images/blog/hey-i-wrote-a-book.webp)

## The Idea

Around 2014, right around the time I switched my college major from general studies to film, my brother David and I started writing a feature-length screenplay called *The Lonely Ponderosa*. I honestly don’t remember whose idea it was. I want to say mine because I love zombie movies and I’ve always thought zombies were the greatest villains. Serial killers, demons, aliens, monsters are all fun to watch, but nothing quite beats taking the people you love and turning them against you. It’s so sick and deranged those zombies. 

Also, the first zombie movie I ever saw was Zack Snyder’s *Dawn of the Dead*. I loved it. It is my favorite zombie movie. There was something horrifyingly intriguing about how fast everything fell apart. One minute people had families and homes and normal lives, and the next everything they knew was trying to eat them. So zombies are my favorite, but it would be hard to pinpoint whose brain the idea actually came from because naturally David also likes zombies. So around 2014, we decided what the world truly needed was zombies, but with Natives and spears and tomahawks and bow and arrows and blood and guts. We couldn't find that version anywhere, so we wrote it.

## The Script

A full feature-length screenplay flew out of our heads and onto the page rather quickly. I think it took us maybe two months total. I kept thinking it would be difficult at some point, but it was really fun. And I loved it. Not only the process, but the end product. And then we discovered something every aspiring filmmaker eventually learns. Nobody wanted to read it. Filmmakers didn’t want to read it. Regular humans didn’t want to read it. We had written this entire movie, and getting someone to open the first page sometimes felt harder than actually making the movie would have been.

But an older gentleman in a nice suit bought me a coffee one day. His name and occupation have unfortunately disappeared completely from my brain. He worked in the film industry, but I can’t remember what exactly he did. Maybe he will find this one day and reach out. Anyways, he completely changed my direction. After hearing that I had written a feature-length screenplay, he bought me a coffee and gave me some advice. He didn’t ask to read the screenplay. He didn’t ask for a logline or a pitch. In fact, he had zero interest in what we had written. Which, looking back, is pretty funny.

Instead, he gave me a piece of advice.

He told me zombies were on their way out, which at that time, they were everywhere. We had *World War Z, GOT, Walking Dead, Z Nation, The Strain*. The undead had eaten their way through popular culture, and audiences were starting to get tired of them. But he told me something else. Everything comes back around. Go work on other things, he said. Make other movies. Write other stories. Learn other beliefs. Research other topics. Then come back to it in a few years. So that’s exactly what we did. 

## The Films

My siblings and I eventually reached a point where we decided waiting for somebody else to tell us whether we were allowed to make movies wasn’t for us. We would write our own screenplays. Make our own films. Figure out our own problems when they popped up. If we didn’t know how to do something, we would youtube. If we couldn’t afford something, we would scrap the scene (or worse use credit). If nobody wanted to give us permission, then screw it. We didn’t need permission.

And somewhere along the way, *The Lonely Ponderosa* went into a drawer. Not literally. It was sitting in a computer somewhere. But it went out of sight, out of mind. Years passed. Films were made. Screenplays written. And then, strangely enough, artificial intelligence brought it all back. Out popped a crazy AI-generated fan made trailer for Brandon Sanderson’s *The Way of Kings*. I immediately thought, “Oh, I’m going to do that for *The Lonely Ponderosa*.” I had the screenplay. I knew exactly what everything looked like in my head. How hard could it be? Turns out pretty hard.

I quickly realized that making an AI-generated trailer look even halfway decent was going to take far more time and money than I initially imagined. What I kept running into was how precise the prompt needed to be. 

## Problem/Solution

The problem wasn’t necessarily the AI. The problem was that the screenplay didn’t contain enough information to explain exactly what I could see in my head. That isn’t a flaw in screenwriting. It is part of the form. A screenplay is a blueprint. You describe what the audience needs to see. Actors bring the characters to life. Costume designers decide what people wear. Production designers build the world. A director decides where the camera goes. Hundreds of people eventually filling in the spaces between the words. And I had been carrying those spaces around in my head for more than a decade. So I started filling them in.

I began turning portions of the screenplay into prose, mostly because I wanted to describe the world more clearly. Then that prose became more prose. And then more prose. Characters started having thoughts. Histories developed between brothers. Relationships became more complicated between lovers. The frontier became larger. The horror became uglier. Places that had existed as a line or two in a screenplay suddenly had histories, textures, smells, weather, people, and memories. The world I had been carrying around in my head spewed out into the real world.

At some point, I stopped adapting a screenplay and realized that I was writing a novel. And the strangest part is that the novel *The Lonely Ponderosa* still contained the bones, ligaments, and blood of the story David and I wrote all those years ago. The book just grew around those things to become something more fully formed.

Maybe that guy in Santa Monica was right. Sometimes a story needs to disappear for a while. You work on other things. You grow up a little (not too much). You get better at your craft. Technology changes. Your life changes. Then one day the story comes wandering back.

In this case, fittingly enough, it came back from the dead.

*The Lonely Ponderosa* began as a screenplay my brother and I couldn’t convince anybody to read.

More than a decade later, it became a novel because I finally realized the story had grown too large to stay trapped inside one.

And now, at long last, it gets to leave my head.
